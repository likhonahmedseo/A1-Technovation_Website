Unmetered Dedicated Server: Is It Truly Unlimited?

An unmetered dedicated server gives you a physical server with covered network traffic that is not billed by the gigabyte or terabyte. It is not truly unlimited. Port speed, network capacity, hardware, and service policies still limit what you can send and receive.

The benefit is easier bandwidth budgeting. A large download or a busy month does not create a per-TB charge for traffic covered by the unmetered agreement. But that agreement needs to match your workload.

Before we compare plans, we need to separate four things: server hardware, connection speed, covered traffic, and usage rules.

What Is an Unmetered Dedicated Server?

An unmetered dedicated server combines exclusive physical hardware with a network plan that does not charge by transfer volume for covered traffic.

The two parts of the name describe different features.

What “dedicated” means

A dedicated server assigns one physical machine to a customer. Its CPU, RAM, and local storage support that customer's applications. The customer may run websites, containers, virtual machines, or other services on it.

Dedicated hardware does not mean every network link between that server and its users is private. Traffic still passes through switches, provider connections, and other networks.

What “unmetered” means

Unmetered describes the traffic billing model. Instead of buying a monthly allowance such as 50TB, you pay for the agreed service, often with a fixed connection rate.

For example, a 1Gbps unmetered plan may let you transfer data without per-TB charges at up to one gigabit per second, subject to the contract. Leaseweb's network billing documentation describes this distinction between transfer allowances and fixed-rate unmetered plans.

The provider can still measure traffic. Monitoring helps it manage network capacity, detect attacks, and enforce service terms. Unmetered billing does not mean unmonitored traffic.

Why Unmetered Does Not Mean Unlimited

An unmetered plan removes a volume-based billing limit for covered traffic. It does not remove the following constraints.

Port speed sets a maximum rate

A 1Gbps connection cannot send more than one gigabit per second in one direction. A 10Gbps connection has ten times that nominal capacity.

Check the configured service rate as well as the network card. A server can have a 10Gbps card while the provider limits its internet connection to 1Gbps.

Also, Gbps means gigabits per second, not gigabytes per second. There are eight bits in a byte. A 1Gbps link therefore equals 125 megabytes per second before network overhead.

Shared network capacity can affect speed

Your server may have its own switch port while sharing upstream capacity with other servers. A busy upstream link can restrict traffic even if your local port has room available.

These three terms need separate checks:

Term What it describes
Dedicated server Hardware allocated to one customer
Dedicated port A network port assigned to that server
Committed bandwidth Capacity covered by a specific service agreement

A bandwidth commitment also has a scope. It may cover the provider's network without promising the same speed to every internet destination.

Usage policies still apply

Some unmetered plans have fair-use rules that restrict sustained high utilization. Others allow continuous use at the contracted rate. The word “unmetered” alone does not tell you which applies.

Fair-use and acceptable-use policies serve different purposes. Fair use may address resource consumption. Acceptable use defines permitted activities and prohibited behavior.

Ask whether your expected traffic is allowed and what happens if you reach a policy threshold. The response could include a warning, reduced speed, an upgrade requirement, or service suspension.

Hardware can become the bottleneck

The network cannot deliver data faster than the server can process or supply it.

A download service may reach its storage read limit before filling a 10Gbps connection. A VPN server may reach its encryption limit. Video transcoding may use all available CPU capacity even when the port is mostly idle.

Unmetered traffic does not increase disk capacity, memory, or processing power.

Unmetered vs Unlimited vs Metered: What Changes?

Metered plans bill or restrict usage by a defined measure. Unmetered plans remove per-volume billing for covered traffic. “Unlimited” requires a closer look at the actual terms.

Plan wording Typical billing approach What still limits use What to check
Metered transfer Monthly allowance, with possible charges for extra data Port rate, allowance, hardware, and network capacity Included TB, counted directions, and overage response
Unmetered traffic Fixed charge for covered traffic Agreed rate, network capacity, hardware, and policies Sustained-use permission and traffic coverage
Unlimited bandwidth Provider-specific wording Physical capacity and contract terms Whether a cap, fair-use threshold, or reduced speed applies

An “unlimited” offer can describe an unmetered service, but the label does not establish its technical limits. Read the plan specification and agreement.

A 95th-percentile plan is another billing model. It uses sampled traffic rates to calculate billable usage under the provider's rules. It is not the same as a fixed-price unmetered plan, and monthly TB alone cannot determine its bill.

How Much Data Can an Unmetered Server Transfer?

A 1Gbps link has a theoretical capacity of 324 TB over 30 days in one direction if it runs at full rate without interruption. A 10Gbps link has a theoretical capacity of 3,240TB over the same period.

These figures show why unmetered does not mean infinite.

The 30-day calculation

For decimal units:

Theoretical transfer in TB = port rate in Gbps × 324

The factor comes from 30 days × 24 hours × 3,600 seconds, with conversion from bits to bytes and then to terabytes.

Connection rate Rate in decimal MB/s before overhead Theoretical transfer over 30 days
100Mbps 12.5 MB/s 32.4 TB
1Gbps 125 MB/s 324 TB
10Gbps 1,250 MB/s 3,240 TB, or 3.24 PB
Assumptions: continuous full-rate use, one traffic direction, decimal units, and 30 days. The table does not deduct protocol overhead or downtime.
30-Day Single-Direction Theoretical Ceiling Decimal Units (Before Overhead)

Why actual file transfer is lower

A connection carries more than the contents of your files. Network headers and other protocol data use part of its capacity. Idle periods, packet loss, congestion, and application limits reduce useful transfer further.

The table is a mathematical ceiling, not a performance promise or a monthly allowance.

Full-duplex networking can send and receive at the same time. It does not turn a 1Gbps outgoing connection into a 2Gbps outgoing connection. Check how the provider defines any combined or per-direction rate limit.

Choose Port Speed From Peak Demand, Not Monthly TB Alone

Monthly transfer tells you how much data moves. Port speed tells you how quickly it can move. You need both figures to choose a server.

Two workloads can transfer the same amount and need different speeds

Consider two illustrative workloads that each send 100TB in a 30-day month.

Traffic pattern Required average rate during transfer
Data leaves evenly throughout the month About 309Mbps
Data leaves during a two-hour window each day About 3.70Gbps

The second workload has only 60 hours to transfer the same data. It needs much more capacity while the transfer runs.

A 1Gbps service may suit the first workload if its peaks, policies, and actual performance fit. It cannot complete the second workload within those windows at that rate. Both calculations exclude overhead and spare capacity.

This is why “we use 100TB per month” is not enough information for a port recommendation.

Check concurrent demand

For direct video delivery, multiply concurrent viewers by the bitrate delivered to each viewer.

200 viewers × 5Mbps = 1,000Mbps

That already reaches the nominal rate of a 1Gbps connection before overhead, other traffic, or spare capacity. This is a sizing example, not a tested server benchmark.

Downloads need a similar check: how many users will download at once, and what rate should each receive? For backups, measure the amount of data that must move within the available window.

When Is an Unmetered Dedicated Server Worth It?

Unmetered hosting is worth considering when you move substantial data and want a predictable charge for that traffic. We would still check the workload's peak rate and hardware needs before choosing a plan.

Workload Why unmetered may help What else determines suitability
Video or audio delivery Sustained outbound traffic can make volume charges costly Bitrate, concurrent users, CDN use, and transcoding capacity
Software and file downloads Releases can create large transfer volumes Storage read speed and peak download demand
Backup transfers Repeated large transfers need a clear bandwidth budget Backup windows, storage writes, and the remote endpoint
VPN services User traffic can create substantial data transfer Permitted use, encryption performance, and routing
CDN origin servers Cache fills and misses can generate large origin transfers Cache behavior and actual origin demand
Game hosting Some services distribute large files or serve many instances CPU performance, latency, packet loss, and attack handling

Game servers do not automatically need unmetered traffic. A small game instance may need strong single-core performance and stable routing more than a large transfer allowance.

CDN users should also separate audience traffic from origin traffic. If a CDN serves a cached file, the origin server does not necessarily send another copy for every download.

An unmetered destination does not remove charges at the other end. For example, moving backups from a cloud service may still create egress charges at that source.

When a Metered Plan May Be the Better Choice

A metered plan can cost less when your usage stays comfortably within its allowance. Small business websites, internal tools, and some database applications may transfer little data despite needing substantial CPU or memory.

Traffic bursts alone do not make unmetered hosting necessary. A metered plan can support a fast port and still include enough monthly transfer for those bursts.

Compare equivalent hardware, port rates, locations, support, and contract periods. Then add the costs that apply to your workload:

  • Expected data-transfer overages.
  • Software and control-panel licenses.
  • Additional IP addresses.
  • Backups and storage.
  • Management and support services.
  • Setup and upgrade fees.

A simple cost comparison

Suppose two otherwise equivalent plans cost $150 and $230 per month. The $150 metered plan includes 50TB and charges $2 for each additional TB. The $230 plan includes unmetered traffic at the required rate.

The $80 price difference equals 40 TB of overage. At 90 TB of billable monthly transfer, both plans cost $230.

These are hypothetical figures, not provider prices. The calculation only applies when overage pricing is linear, and the services otherwise match. Confirm whether the allowance counts inbound traffic, outbound traffic, or both.

What Should You Check Before Ordering?

A useful quote should answer more than “Is this server unmetered?” We recommend getting the following details in writing.

1

Which traffic directions are covered?

Inbound traffic, or ingress, enters your server. Outbound traffic, or egress, leaves it.

A plan with unmetered inbound traffic and a 50TB outbound allowance still limits billable outgoing volume. That distinction matters for streaming, downloads, and VPN services.

2

What rate can the service use?

Confirm the physical port rate, configured service rate, and any bandwidth commitment. Ask where that commitment applies and whether it covers sustained use.

3

What happens during sustained high usage?

Describe your expected average and peak traffic. Ask whether continuous use at the agreed rate is permitted and whether fair-use thresholds apply.

4

Is the workload permitted?

Check acceptable-use rules for your application. A bandwidth plan does not automatically authorize every type of VPN, proxy, file distribution, or other service.

5

How does the provider handle attacks?

Unmetered traffic does not itself include DDoS protection. Ask what protection covers, whether charges can apply, and whether the provider may temporarily block traffic to your IP during an attack.

6

What can still increase the bill?

Check management, backups, licenses, IP addresses, upgrades, and services outside the bandwidth agreement. A predictable traffic charge is only one part of the total hosting cost.

Use this table to question unclear wording:

Advertised phrase Question to ask
“Up to 10Gbps” What rate is configured, and is any capacity committed?
“Unlimited traffic” Are there transfer thresholds, fair-use rules, or speed reductions?
“Dedicated port” What bandwidth commitment applies beyond the server's switch port?
“Unmetered inbound” How is outbound traffic billed?
“DDoS protection included” What limits and responses apply during an attack?

How to Check Whether the Service Meets Your Needs

Test the service against the workload you expect to run. A single short speed test cannot establish all-day performance to every destination.

Ask for a test IP, a network looking glass, or permission to run throughput tests against agreed endpoints. A looking glass provides a way to inspect network routes and reachability from the provider's network.

During evaluation, record:

  • Inbound and outbound transfer rates.
  • Performance during your users' busy hours.
  • Latency and packet loss to relevant destinations.
  • CPU use during encryption or other processing.
  • Storage read and write rates during transfers.

If transfers slow down, compare these measurements. A full CPU core, slow storage, a configured rate limit, and a congested route need different fixes.

Continue monitoring after deployment. Use the results to plan upgrades before the service runs out of usable capacity.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a provider monitor an unmetered server's traffic?

Yes. Unmetered describes billing, not the absence of measurement. Providers may track traffic rates and totals for network management, security, and policy enforcement.

Is unmetered bandwidth guaranteed bandwidth?

No. Unmetered concerns charges for transfer volume. Guaranteed or committed bandwidth concerns the capacity promised under a service agreement. A plan can include both, but one does not establish the other.

Does a 10Gbps port include unlimited monthly data?

No. A 10Gbps port describes a connection rate. The plan may include a fixed transfer allowance or unmetered traffic. Check the bandwidth package separately from the port specification.

Can an unmetered server be throttled?

It can, depending on the agreement. Providers may enforce configured rates or apply policy-based restrictions. Ask what triggers a speed change and whether sustained use at the contracted rate is allowed.

Does unmetered cover uploads and downloads?

Only if both directions fall within the agreement. Some plans treat inbound and outbound traffic differently. Get confirmation for the selected server and location.

Does unmetered hosting include unlimited storage?

No. Storage capacity remains limited by the installed drives and any separate storage service. CPU, RAM, backup capacity, and network traffic are also separate resources.

Can we host multiple websites on one unmetered dedicated server?

Yes, provided the hardware and software support their combined needs. Plan for CPU use, RAM, storage, security, and licensing. Unmetered traffic does not remove those requirements.

Is an unmetered VPS the same as an unmetered dedicated server?

No. Both may offer unmetered traffic, but a VPS runs as a virtual server on a host. A dedicated server assigns the physical machine to one customer. Compare CPU allocation, network limits, storage performance, and traffic terms separately.
